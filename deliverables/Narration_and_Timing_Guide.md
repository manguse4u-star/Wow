1. (0:25–0:31) Point to the ridge and the stripes.
2. (0:31–0:41) Pull both strips outward: new paper at the ridge is new crust, and each stripe records the field polarity when that crust cooled.
3. (0:41–0:47) Show the matching patterns on both sides.
4. (0:47–0:56) Worked example: "If this stripe is 100 km from the ridge and 5 million years old, the half-spreading rate is 100 km ÷ 5 Myr = 2 cm per year" (100 km = 10,000,000 cm; ÷ 5,000,000 yr = 2 cm/yr).
5. (0:56–1:00) Short conclusion, e.g. "So the stripes let us read how fast the ocean floor moves."

## Scientific notes (why the diagrams are drawn this way)
- **Seafloor depth and lithosphere thickness increase with distance from the ridge** along a square-root curve, matching the observed depth ∝ √age cooling relationship (Parsons & Sclater, 1977). This is why the ridge stands high.
- **Magma**: shown as a partial-melt zone that narrows up toward the axis. It is produced by decompression melting of upwelling mantle, which is passive: the ridge is not a piston pushing the plates. New crust appears *only at the axis*, and existing crust is carried away rigidly. No old crust re-emerges from the mantle.
- **Half-spreading rate** is drawn from the ridge axis (rate relative to the ridge). **Full-spreading rate** is drawn plate-to-plate (relative separation). Full = 2 × half *only* for symmetric spreading, which the caption states. Real ridges can be asymmetric (Müller et al., 2008).
- **Stripes** are colour-coded by **magnetic polarity**, with an explicit legend. They are not rock colours or rock types. The pattern is mirrored about the ridge (Vine & Matthews, 1963). Rates come from combining stripe distances with the dated geomagnetic polarity timescale.
